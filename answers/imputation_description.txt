Imputation Approach — AAVAIL Missing Data

My investigation showed two distinct missingness patterns, so I used two different imputation strategies rather than one blanket approach. The age, gender, and monthly_charges columns were missing at low, fairly uniform rates (3-7%) across every country and subscriber plan, which is consistent with Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) data — most likely simple non-response or minor data-entry gaps — so I imputed these with straightforward statistics (median for the numeric age field, mode for the categorical gender field, and a plan-level median for monthly_charges, since price is tightly coupled to subscriber_type). In contrast, num_streams and customer_service_calls were missing much more often (10% and 7% overall) and unevenly: num_streams was missing far more frequently for "basic" and "student" plans, and customer_service_calls was missing more often for longer-tenure customers, indicating Missing At Random (MAR) data whose missingness depends on other observed fields rather than pure chance. For these two columns I used group-wise median imputation — grouping by subscriber_type for num_streams and by tenure quartile for customer_service_calls — so that the imputed values reflect the typical usage/support-call behavior of similar customers instead of pulling every customer toward one global average, which would have flattened real differences between plan tiers and customer tenures. After imputation, the overall means and standard deviations for each column changed only marginally versus the non-missing subset, which supports that the chosen imputation method preserved the original distribution shape rather than introducing bias.
